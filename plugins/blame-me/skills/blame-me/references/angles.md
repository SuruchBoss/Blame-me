# Places to look

Use the ones that fit the work. Not every one fits every piece. Skip the ones that do not, and never pad a report to cover them all.

1. **The problem and who has it.** Who has this problem, and what in the work shows it is real (a quote from a real person, a count, a story)? Is the person with the problem the same person who pays?

2. **Why this, and not what people use now.** People already deal with the problem somehow: a free app, a notebook, asking a friend, or doing nothing. Does the work say what that is and why someone would switch? "No competitors" almost always means the real competitor was missed.

3. **Money.** Redo every sum. For every percentage or average, check what it is a percentage of. Put together numbers that sit in different parts: cost of one item × how many, against the price; number of people × pay, against the budget. Can the cost and profit of one sale be rebuilt from what the work says? If the work does not give enough to rebuild it, that is a weak spot too.

4. **Getting the first customers or users.** Where do the first ten come from, by name or by place? What does it cost to win one? "1% of the market" is a hope, not a plan.

5. **Dates and steps.** Do the dates in the summary match the dates in the plan? Is the order possible (selling before the product exists, spending money before it arrives)? Is there time for anything to go wrong? For one person or a small team: are there enough hours in the day for what the plan says they will do?

6. **People.** Who has to change what they do? Who loses something if this works (a shop nearby, another department, the person who does the job now)? Who has to say yes, and does the work talk to that person?

7. **Risks and rules.** Name a specific rule only when you are sure of it. When you are not sure, write it under Needs checking ("Needs checking: whether selling food on campus needs permission from the university"). Never state a law, a fee, or a rule from memory as fact.

8. **Reading it for the first time.** Does the first page say what this is and why the reader should care? Do two parts disagree? Does it mention a page, appendix, or picture that is not there? Are words used before they are explained? Would someone who knows nothing about it know where to start?

9. **Why now.** What changes if this waits six months? What if someone bigger does the same thing next month?

10. **Claims about the team or the author.** If the work says "our team can do this" or "I led X", does the work show it? Judge only what the work says and shows. Do not judge the person.

For a resume, application, or case study, number 10 becomes: are the claims backed by results or numbers, and do they match what is asked for? Number 3 becomes: are the numbers given (savings, growth, team size) believable and consistent with each other?

## What already holds up

While looking, note what survives. Examples:

- a sum that is correct when redone;
- a claim the work backs with something real (a count it made, a named customer, a real quote);
- a part that answers exactly what the brief or criteria ask.

Each one says what was checked. Do not list things that were not tested ("the design is nice"). If nothing survives, say so.

## What is not a weak spot

- Advice that fits any work: "add more data", "think about your audience", "could be shorter".
- Taste: "I would use a different colour", unless it stops a reader understanding.
- Anything about the author's personality, motives, age, background outside the work, or private life.
- A guess presented as a fact. Move it to Needs checking.
